Derive a distinctive visual direction from both the source site's visible identity and the source assessment. Treat the site's brand colors, logo, photography, language, and visual motifs as the primary evidence. Use industry conventions as a secondary cue: for example, a mechanic may call for a sturdy, workmanlike feel, while a landscaping company may call for a more natural palette and imagery. Adapt those cues to the actual source; do not turn them into stereotypes or assume an industry, audience, palette, typeface, mood, location, or conversion goal in advance.

Translate that evidence into a coherent visual system: an intentional palette that respects or thoughtfully refreshes the source colors, type pairing, spacing, image treatment, and one or two relevant motifs. Preserve recognizable brand cues where supported, while making the redesign feel intentional and current rather than copying the existing page.

Choose a section order and composition suited to this organization and its conversion path. Use varied, purposeful layouts, a clear hierarchy, readable typography, and a compelling image-led hero. Avoid defaulting to repeated card grids, generic gradients, decorative effects, or filler sections. Include only content sections that make sense for the source and supported facts.

Use the organization's own logo, photography, and visual assets when accessible and suitable. Match image subjects to the organization and the section they support. Choose the primary CTA based on the actual audience and goal; make it prominent and keep its label free of arrows or directional icons.

Design mobile intentionally: adapt navigation, image crops, content order, spacing, and CTA prominence for a small screen. Keep the page accessible, keyboard usable, and readable at all viewport sizes. Use a named HTML5 UP reference as structural inspiration only when the source assessment finds one that genuinely fits; otherwise create an original direction without forcing a template.
